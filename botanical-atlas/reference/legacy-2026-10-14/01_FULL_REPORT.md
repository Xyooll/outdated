# Worldwide Botanical & Fungal Identity Atlas — Example Research Batch

**Batch:** 2026-10-14 • **32 distinct organisms (24 plants, 8 fungi)** • **Status:** worked example / starter index, not exhaustive chemical monographs.

**Evidence labels:** Human = human study; Preclinical = cells/animals; Traditional = recorded cultural use; Hypothesis = untested combination. A molecule being detected does not establish it reaches the target tissue at biologically relevant concentration.

**Four domains:** B metabolic/bioenergetic; P somatic/physiological; C cognitive; A affective/emotional. Effects can overlap, but blank/negative findings must be recorded.

**Safety:** Research catalog, not consumption, extraction, medical treatment or compound-synthesis instructions. High-dose essential oils, poorly identified mushrooms, and homemade mixtures can be dangerous.

# BOT-001 — Clove (Syzygium aromaticum)

**Identity / provenance:** Maluku Islands, Indonesia; used throughout Asia, Africa and elsewhere  
**Part and preparation scope:** dried flower buds; oil is not equivalent to whole buds

## Known / reported priority compounds

- **Eugenol** — dominant volatile; affects pain signaling and microbial membranes in laboratory settings.
- **Eugenyl acetate** — aromatic ester and potential eugenol precursor; human role poorly isolated.
- **β-caryophyllene** — terpene with CB2 receptor activity shown experimentally.
- **α-humulene** — sesquiterpene with preclinical anti-inflammatory effects.
- **Gallic acid** — phenolic antioxidant in laboratory models.
- **Quercetin** — flavonoid modulating inflammatory signaling preclinically.
- **Kaempferol** — flavonoid studied for cell-signaling effects.
- **Ellagic acid** — tannin-related polyphenol.
- **Caffeic acid** — common plant phenolic.

## Four-domain effects (not all clinically proven)

- **B – bioenergetic/metabolic:** No demonstrated meaningful energy increase from culinary use.
- **P – physiological:** Local dental numbing has a plausible eugenol mechanism; lab antibacterial/antifungal findings do not prove infection treatment.
- **C – cognitive/neurocognitive:** No reliable human memory improvement established.
- **A – affective/psychophysiological:** Aromatic sensory effects are possible; antidepressant claims are unproved.

## Evidence level and limits

Composition well characterized; topical/dental use more established than systemic clinical claims.

## Global / traditional context

Indonesian spice tradition; Dingxiang (丁香) in Chinese materia medica for cold-type digestive symptoms.

## Combination map / identity links

BOT-001 ↔ BOT-012 (eugenol shared); BOT-001 ↔ BOT-005 (eugenol shared, especially cinnamon leaf); BOT-001 ↔ BOT-002 (overlapping membrane-active aromatic phenols; synergy unproven).

## Safety and unknowns

Concentrated clove oil can burn tissue; swallowing amounts can cause severe liver injury, especially in children; allergy possible.

# BOT-002 — Thyme (Thymus vulgaris)

**Identity / provenance:** Mediterranean; European/Middle Eastern herbal traditions  
**Part and preparation scope:** leaf and flowering tops; chemotypes differ markedly

## Known / reported priority compounds

- **Thymol** — volatile phenol, disrupts microbial membranes in vitro.
- **Carvacrol** — thymol isomer with similar lab antimicrobial activity.
- **p-Cymene** — aromatic terpene, often a co-component.
- **γ-Terpinene** — terpene precursor and aroma component.
- **Rosmarinic acid** — polyphenol with preclinical inflammatory signaling effects.
- **Luteolin** — flavone studied for anti-inflammatory actions.
- **Apigenin** — flavone studied for nervous-system interactions.

## Four-domain effects (not all clinically proven)

- **B – bioenergetic/metabolic:** Human metabolic effects inadequately measured.
- **P – physiological:** Traditional cough/digestive applications; antimicrobial effects mostly laboratory evidence.
- **C – cognitive/neurocognitive:** No confirmed cognition outcome.
- **A – affective/psychophysiological:** Aroma may feel soothing; not clinically established anxiolytic therapy.

## Evidence level and limits

Chemistry strong; clinical results depend on extract and indication.

## Global / traditional context

Mediterranean household herb and European herbal medicines.

## Combination map / identity links

BOT-002 ↔ BOT-004 (rosmarinic acid); BOT-002 ↔ BOT-012 (aromatic-phenol overlap, not proven synergy).

## Safety and unknowns

Essential oil can irritate mucosa/skin; do not assume undiluted oil is safe to ingest.

## Sources for research verification

# BOT-003 — Rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus (syn. Rosmarinus officinalis))

- **C – cognitive/neurocognitive:** Small fragrance studies suggest possible attention associations; no reliable memory treatment.
- **A – affective/psychophysiological:** Aroma may influence subjective alertness.

## Evidence level and limits

Chemistry well characterized; most compound mechanisms are nonhuman.

## Global / traditional context

Mediterranean kitchen/ritual fragrance; global medicinal use.

## Combination map / identity links

BOT-003 ↔ BOT-004 and BOT-012 (rosmarinic acid); BOT-003 ↔ BOT-016 (ursolic acid in other plants).

## Safety and unknowns

# BOT-004 — Peppermint (Mentha × piperita)

**Identity / provenance:** Europe; later widespread  
**Part and preparation scope:** leaves, steam-distilled oil; enteric-coated oil researched separately from leaf tea

## Known / reported priority compounds

- **Menthol** — activates cold-sensing TRPM8 and relaxes intestinal smooth muscle.
- **Menthone** — major volatile ketone and possible contributor.
- **Menthyl acetate** — volatile ester, minor individual evidence.
- **1,8-Cineole** — aromatic terpene.
- **Menthofuran** — volatile compound monitored for oil quality.
- **Rosmarinic acid** — leaf phenolic mainly in non-oil extracts.
- **Pulegone** — potentially hepatotoxic constituent controlled in quality standards.

## Four-domain effects (not all clinically proven)

- **B – bioenergetic/metabolic:** No persuasive energy effect.
- **P – physiological:** Enteric-coated peppermint oil has mixed but modestly favorable short-term IBS evidence.
- **C – cognitive/neurocognitive:** Cooling sensation is not brain-cooling or memory improvement.
- **A – affective/psychophysiological:** Comfort from relieved gut symptoms may affect mood indirectly.

## Evidence level and limits

Human clinical trials for formulated oil, evidence low/very low quality overall.

## Global / traditional context

Ancient Egyptian, Greek and European digestive traditions.

## Combination map / identity links

BOT-004 ↔ BOT-003/BOT-012 share rosmarinic acid, but not necessarily in their oils.

## Safety and unknowns

Can worsen reflux; concentrates can irritate; high pulegone exposure is unsafe.

# BOT-005 — Ceylon cinnamon (Cinnamomum verum)

**Identity / provenance:** Sri Lanka and South Asia; traded globally  
**Part and preparation scope:** inner bark; leaf oil has different chemistry from bark oil

## Known / reported priority compounds

- **trans-Cinnamaldehyde** — primary bark aroma, reacts with proteins and affects microbial/ metabolic signaling in labs.
- **Eugenol** — much richer in leaf oil, see BOT-001.
- **Procyanidins** — polyphenol oligomers studied for metabolic effects.
- **Cinnamic acid** — aromatic acid.
- **Cinnamyl acetate** — aroma ester.
- **Coumarin** — far lower in Ceylon than most cassia species, a critical identity marker.

## Four-domain effects (not all clinically proven)

- **B – bioenergetic/metabolic:** Blood-glucose research across cinnamon species is inconsistent; do not transfer findings automatically.
- **P – physiological:** Antimicrobial effects often test concentrated extracts, not food portions.
- **C – cognitive/neurocognitive:** No convincing cognitive improvement.
- **A – affective/psychophysiological:** Aroma has sensory associations, not clinical mood treatment.

## Evidence level and limits

Human studies are heterogeneous and often mix cinnamon species.

## Global / traditional context

Sri Lankan culinary and South/West Asian medicinal traditions.

## Combination map / identity links

BOT-005 ↔ BOT-001 via eugenol, principally cinnamon LEAF oil; BOT-005 ↔ BOT-023 possible shared glycemic pathway, not demonstrated synergy.

## Safety and unknowns

Cassia substitutions can have much higher coumarin and liver risk; oil can irritate.

# BOT-006 — Turmeric (Curcuma longa)

**Identity / provenance:** South Asia; Ayurveda and regional food traditions  
**Part and preparation scope:** rhizome; curcuminoid extracts differ from turmerone-rich oil

## Known / reported priority compounds

- **Curcumin** — polyphenol studied for inflammatory signaling, but has low oral bioavailability.

## Evidence level and limits

# BOT-007 — Ginger (Zingiber officinale)

**Identity / provenance:** South and Southeast Asia; Ayurveda, Chinese and other traditions  
**Part and preparation scope:** fresh/dried rhizome; drying/heating changes gingerols to shogaols

## Known / reported priority compounds

- **6-Gingerol** — pungent phenolic studied for gastrointestinal signaling and inflammation.
- **8-Gingerol** — related homologue with limited human isolation data.
- **10-Gingerol** — longer-chain homologue.
- **6-Shogaol** — dehydration product enriched by drying/heat, potent preclinical signaling.
- **Zingerone** — less pungent aromatic breakdown product.
- **Zingiberene** — volatile sesquiterpene aroma.

## Four-domain effects (not all clinically proven)

- **B – bioenergetic/metabolic:** Glucose/metabolic claims are not consistently established.
- **P – physiological:** Human evidence supports ginger for some nausea contexts, especially pregnancy-associated nausea; not all types of vomiting.
- **C – cognitive/neurocognitive:** No established cognition enhancement.
- **A – affective/psychophysiological:** May improve well-being indirectly by decreasing nausea.

## Evidence level and limits

Whole ginger studied in people; individual gingerol action primarily laboratory-based.

## Global / traditional context

Ginger used in Asian medicine for digestive cold and nausea.

## Combination map / identity links

BOT-007 ↔ BOT-004 both target gut symptoms through different mechanisms; combination effect needs study.

## Safety and unknowns

High-intake supplements may aggravate reflux and interact with medicines.

# BOT-008 — Garlic (Allium sativum)

**Identity / provenance:** Central Asia origin; global food and medicine  
**Part and preparation scope:** bulb; chopping generates allicin, aging creates a different profile

## Known / reported priority compounds

- **Alliin** — stable precursor converted by alliinase upon crushing.
- **Allicin** — reactive sulfur compound with laboratory antimicrobial actions, unstable.
- **Ajoene** — sulfur-derived molecule formed from allicin, platelet effects studied experimentally.
- **Diallyl disulfide** — volatile sulfur compound with preclinical signaling effects.
- **Diallyl trisulfide** — sulfur compound studied for vascular signaling.
- **S-Allyl cysteine** — more prominent in aged garlic extracts and studied in human formulations.

## Four-domain effects (not all clinically proven)

- **B – bioenergetic/metabolic:** Some formulated supplements show modest blood-pressure or lipid marker changes.
- **P – physiological:** Vascular effects studied clinically; topical antimicrobial lab results do not justify applying garlic to wounds.
- **C – cognitive/neurocognitive:** No clear clinical memory benefit.
- **A – affective/psychophysiological:** No direct mood efficacy established.

## Evidence level and limits

Human clinical studies on selected garlic preparations; chemistry dramatically preparation-dependent.

## Global / traditional context

Global culinary/folk medicine, including South and East Asian systems.

## Combination map / identity links

BOT-008 ↔ BOT-019 blood-pressure pathways might add; not safe to assume clinically.

## Safety and unknowns

Bleeding interaction concerns and surgery precautions; raw topical garlic can severely burn skin.

# BOT-009 — Green tea (Camellia sinensis)

**Identity / provenance:** China origin; East Asian and worldwide tea cultures  
**Part and preparation scope:** minimally oxidized leaves; brewed tea versus high-dose extracts

## Known / reported priority compounds

- **EGCG** — major catechin; affects oxidative/inflammatory pathways in vitro.
- **EGC** — related catechin.
- **ECG** — gallated catechin.
- **EC** — catechin.
- **L-Theanine** — amino acid associated with attentional and relaxation effects in some human studies.
- **Caffeine** — adenosine receptor antagonist, reliably increases wakefulness.
- **Quercetin** — minor flavonoid depending on product.

## Four-domain effects (not all clinically proven)

- **B – bioenergetic/metabolic:** Caffeine acutely increases perceived energy, not cellular ATP production; other metabolic effects generally modest.
- **P – physiological:** Some lipid/vascular effects; concentrated catechin exposure can harm liver.
- **C – cognitive/neurocognitive:** Caffeine raises alertness; theanine/caffeine combinations studied for attention.
- **A – affective/psychophysiological:** Caffeine can worsen anxiety; theanine may soften subjective tension in some contexts.

## Evidence level and limits

Strong caffeine evidence, moderate/variable human trials for tea mixtures, mechanistic for catechins.

## Global / traditional context

Tea cultures in China, Japan, Korea and beyond.

## Combination map / identity links

BOT-009 ↔ BOT-024 coffee shares caffeine; caffeine + theanine are an investigated functional combination within tea.

## Safety and unknowns

Extracts are riskier than customary brewed tea; sleep, drug and iron-absorption cautions.

# BOT-013 — Asian ginseng (Panax ginseng)
