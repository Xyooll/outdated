1. Medicinal perspectives

Ding Xiang · 丁香 · Traditional Chinese Medicinesubstantial existing

Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020 · documented traditional framework

Material
Caryophylli Flos: dried unopened flower bud; not clove essential oil
Nature and flavor
温 (warm), 辛 (pungent/acrid)
Channel affinities
脾 (Spleen), 胃 (Stomach), 肺 (Lung), 肾 (Kidney) in the pharmacopoeia; a practitioner source omits Lung.
Actions
温中降逆 (warm middle, descend counterflow Qi); 补肾助阳 (assist Kidney Yang)
Indication patterns
Spleen–Stomach deficiency-cold, hiccup/vomiting counterflow, cold-pattern abdominal discomfort, Kidney-Yang deficiency descriptions.
Traditional cautions
Sources caution on heat-pattern use and classical incompatibility with Yu Jin (Curcumae Radix). Neither proves a laboratory interaction or clinical effect.
Historical amount
1–3 g for dried bud in cited monograph; not a personalized safe dose and never transferable to oil.
Research gap
Classical formula comparisons, processing variants, source-page verification, regional/school subprofiles and clinical differentiation.

Sources: Ding Xiang 2020 Chinese Pharmacopoeia reference (existing Atlas source)

Lavanga · लवङ्ग · Ayurvedasource backed seed

Indian Ayurvedic materia medica · documented traditional framework

Names
Lavanga, Devakusuma; modern sources refer to Syzygium aromaticum flower buds.
Rasa
Katu (pungent), Tikta (bitter), with classical lexicon-specific differences.
Guna
Laghu (light), Snigdha (unctuous) in the Ayurvedic college summary; other texts differ, including Ruksha/Tikshna variants.
Virya / Vipaka
Shita (cooling potency) in the cited college account; Katu postdigestive effect there, while the 2019 multi-text review records disagreement about Vipaka. Do not force an 'always warming' TCM equivalence.
Dosha and actions
The review describes Kapha/Pitta-regulating claims, Deepana, Pachana, Ruchya, and other traditional actions; this is an internal Ayurvedic framework, not proof of physiology.
Historical indications
Trishna (thirst), Chhardi (vomiting), Hikka (hiccup), Shoola (pain), Kasa/Shwasa (cough/breathing complaints), digestive distress described traditionally.
Historical formulas
Names include Lavangadi Churna, Lavangadi Vati, Lavangachatussama; constituent ratios and outcomes need independent investigation.
Safety and disagreement
Sources even disagree on reported amounts and properties. No quoted oil-drop recommendation is an established safe dose.
Research gap
Primary passage verification, mapped passages from individual nighantus, botanical part identity, and trial-level evidence.

Sources: Pamnani et al., A Comprehensive Ayurvedic Review on Lavanga, AYUSHDHARA 2019 · Shri Dhanwantry Ayurvedic College, Lavanga materia medica summary

Qaranfal · قرنفل · Unani/Tibbsource backed seed

Greco-Arabic and South Asian Unani · documented traditional framework

Mizaj
Harr/Yabis — hot and dry, often third degree in the cited review; degree belongs to the attributed author and should not be generalized.
Afa'al (reported actions)
Musakkin-e-Alam (analgesic), Mohallil-e-Waram (anti-inflammatory), Mufarreh and Muqawwi-e-Meda (stomachic) reported by sources; traditional terms are not clinical verification.
Historical indications
Toothache, halitosis, flatulence/colic, dyspepsia, and other Unani uses in review literature.
Preparation context
Dried Qaranfal in multi-ingredient formulas and aromatic oil are not equivalent. Historic formula provenance requires ingredient-specific source links.
Clinical contrast
A small open study with 20 people assessed a **combination** containing cloves for allergic rhinitis; neither efficacy nor cloves' isolated contribution was established.
Research gap
Original pharmacopeial passages, precise mizaj disputes, dose/processing variability, contraindications by school, high-quality controlled trials.

Sources: Role of Unani Medicines in Oro-Dental Diseases: A Comprehensive Review and Scientific Evidence · Latafat & Kidwai, Itrifal ustukhudoos plus cloves in allergic rhinitis, preliminary open study

Western biomedicine · evidence-based outcomessource backed seed

Modern experimental and clinical research · mixed in vitro and safety review

Identification and exposure
Analytic chemistry from a particular bud, stem, leaf or oil sample is required before attributing a measured constituent percentage.
Dental and local anesthetic context
Eugenol has recognized local dental applications, but raw concentrated clove oil can injure mucosa and must not be treated as an interchangeable dental drug.
Antiparasitic laboratory findings
Published cell/parasite assays report activity on Giardia, Leishmania and Haemonchus stages. These do not establish a human or veterinary antiparasitic treatment.
Other preclinical findings
Clove oil and eugenol show antimicrobial, antioxidant and other experimental outcomes, often in vitro; no generic 'cures infection' conclusion.
Safety
Concentrated oil ingestion can cause severe organ toxicity, particularly in children; sensitization and oral irritation possible. Interactions need route/dose-specific evidence.
Research gap
Controlled clinical outcome syntheses, absorption and tissue exposure, preparation identity, toxicology thresholds, negative trials and pharmacovigilance.

Sources: Haro-González et al., Clove Essential Oil: Extraction, Chemical Composition and Bioactivity, Molecules 2021 · Anti-Giardia clove oil and eugenol assays · Eugenol effects on Leishmania mexicana in vitro · Eugenol assays versus Haemonchus contortus · LiverTox: Eugenol (Clove Oil)

2. Metaphysical and symbolic traditions

Devakusuma and auspicious-flower epithetssource backed seed

Sanskrit materia medica lexicon histories · historical secondary review not spiritual proof

Documented language
The 2019 review attributes the names Devakusuma (flower associated with gods) and Shreesangya (auspicious) to named traditional lexicons.
Meaning and caution
These are text-reported epithets, not evidence that clove has a universal planetary, elemental or chakra correspondence, nor proof of ritual effects.
Research gap
Verify exact original texts, translation, date and whether entries refer to clove across historical botanical naming.

Sources: Pamnani et al., A Comprehensive Ayurvedic Review on Lavanga, AYUSHDHARA 2019

Aromatic custom and pomanderssource backed seed

Historical European scented-object practice · documented folklore historical custom

Historical record
Greenwich Historical Society describes medieval aromatic sachets and later clove-studded citrus pomanders as fragrance customs.
Belief context
Historical communities associated bad smells with illness; this represents historical disease theory, not evidence fragrant objects prevent infection.
Scope
Pomander history is cultural/symbolic and not automatically a magical protection ritual. The modern citrus-clove holiday decoration has later Victorian associations.
Research gap
Find dated primary accounts of specific ritual, spiritual, protection or planetary symbolism; do not conflate all European eras.

Sources: Greenwich Historical Society: Pomander Balls, historical aromatic customs

3. Active ingredients and experiments

Eugenol · dominant volatile constituentsource backed seed

Analytical chemistry and in-vitro pharmacology · analytical and in vitro

Identity
Eugenol · C₁₀H₁₂O₂ · PubChem CID 3314 · CAS 97-53-0; clove's existing molecular SVG is stored in structures/CMP-eugenol.svg.
Chemistry
A phenylpropanoid phenol; not the same as eugenyl acetate or clove essential-oil mixture.
Quantitative example
A cited GC-MS study reports 79.21% eugenol, 11.60% β-caryophyllene, 6.27% eugenyl acetate and 1.56% humulene **within that studied oil**, not universally across cloves.
Study effects
Existing Atlas includes in-vitro Giardia, Leishmania, Haemonchus activity with parasite stage and concentration; several of clove's 11 occurrence records are legacy-unreviewed.
Evidence warning
Observed lab activity ≠ patient benefit, achievable dose, or preparation-level efficacy. A compound may be toxic at active concentrations.
Research gap
Concentration by organ and harvest batch, full minor-metabolite verification, receptor causality, pharmacokinetics and human outcomes.

Sources: PubChem Eugenol CID 3314 · Evaluating the multifaceted bioactivity of Syzygium aromaticum essential oil · Anti-Giardia clove oil and eugenol assays · Eugenol effects on Leishmania mexicana in vitro · Eugenol assays versus Haemonchus contortus

Other constituent familiessource backed seed

Clove oil chemistry · analytical review

Major volatile co-constituents
β-Caryophyllene, eugenyl acetate and α-humulene repeatedly reported, with strong method- and part-specific variability.
Non-volatile compounds
Reported tannins/polyphenols (e.g., gallic acid and flavonoids) depend on material and extraction; they should not inherit bud-oil percentage estimates.
Coverage limitation
The existing Atlas associates 11 compounds with clove, but many rows have unresolved original references. This is a partial inventory, not 'all active ingredients'.

Sources: Haro-González et al., Clove Essential Oil: Extraction, Chemical Composition and Bioactivity, Molecules 2021

4. Extraction and preparation

Hydrodistillationsource backed seed

Experimental essential-oil extraction · comparative method review

Input → output
Clove buds or other named parts → mixed volatile essential oil. This is not purified eugenol.
Method scope
Water/heat-driven volatility and condensation, with reported GC-MS comparisons among batches.
Measured outcomes
Reviewed studies report differing major-oil percentages by method and sampling; sample-specific 69.68–87.26% eugenol entries appear in the cited review, not a guaranteed yield.
Hazards
Heat and concentrated oil exposure; extracted oils are not validated as oral antiparasitic medicines.
Research gap
Source study metadata and normalized mass-balance/yield by plant part, pre-treatment and batch.

Sources: Haro-González et al., Clove Essential Oil: Extraction, Chemical Composition and Bioactivity, Molecules 2021

Steam distillationsource backed seed

Experimental and industrial essential-oil extraction · comparative method review

Input → output
Volatile-bearing clove materials → steam-distilled aromatic oil.
Comparison
Different raw plant parts, steam handling and storage produce differing profiles; an oil label is insufficient to establish purity.
Research gap
Extract specific studies and explicit quantitative comparisons with hydrodistillation.

Sources: Haro-González et al., Clove Essential Oil: Extraction, Chemical Composition and Bioactivity, Molecules 2021

Solvent and ultrasound-assisted extractionssource backed seed

Laboratory botanical extraction · comparative method review

Input → output
Ground or intact clove buds and a specified solvent/media → multi-constituent extract rather than essential oil by definition.
Technique diversity
The cited review surveys solvent extraction and ultrasound-assisted techniques that change recovered constituent proportions.
Hazards
Solvent residue, flammability and extract concentration; a laboratory procedure is not a home-use recommendation.
Research gap
Independently validated solvent residual tests, batch yields, precise eugenol isolation vs crude-extract identity.

Sources: Haro-González et al., Clove Essential Oil: Extraction, Chemical Composition and Bioactivity, Molecules 2021

Supercritical fluid and microwave-assisted methodssource backed seed

Engineered extraction technologies · comparative method review

Input → output
Clove material → method-specific volatile fractions or extracts, contingent on analytical results.
Method classes
Supercritical-fluid extraction and microwave-assisted hydrodistillation are reviewed; they differ from traditional water infusions.
Research gap
Reproducible method-specific comparisons with emissions, residues, quality, purification and cost.

Sources: Haro-González et al., Clove Essential Oil: Extraction, Chemical Composition and Bioactivity, Molecules 2021
